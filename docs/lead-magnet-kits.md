## Kit 1 — Evidence Intake & Chronology Readiness Kit (attorneys)

**Subtitle:** A short, practical checklist for taking on a documentation-heavy domestic-violence or coercive-control case.

### 1. Client intake — what to ask for in the first meeting

- A rough timeline in the client's own words, even if incomplete or out of order. Don't wait for a polished version.
- Every device and account that might hold evidence: phone, old phones, email, cloud photo backups, shared family accounts, smart-home apps, car apps.
- Names of anyone who witnessed an incident or its aftermath (a neighbor, a coworker, a responding officer), even if the client isn't sure they'd testify.
- Any existing paper trail: police reports, medical records, prior protective orders, school or custody-evaluation records, text or email exports the client has already saved.
- Ask explicitly whether the client has been documenting on their own before retaining you — many have a phone full of screenshots with no structure. That material is often usable; it just needs organizing.

### 2. Building a chronology that holds up

- Every entry needs three things at minimum: a date (or a stated approximate date — "early March" is better than nothing), a description in the client's own words, and a link back to the source (the actual text, photo, or document it came from).
- Resist the urge to summarize away the client's original language in early drafts. Paraphrase later; preserve the source first.
- Approximate dates are normal and don't need to be hidden — mark them as approximate rather than guessing a specific date you can't support.
- When the same event shows up in two places (a text message and a police report, say), link them to each other. Corroboration is often the difference between an allegation and a documented pattern.

### 3. Evidence handling basics

- Preserve originals before anything else — a screenshot of a screenshot loses metadata. If the client still has the original message/photo/file, get that first.
- Note where each piece of evidence came from and when it was captured, even informally. You don't need forensic chain-of-custody for every item, but you do need to be able to say where it came from if asked.
- Don't edit or crop images before preserving a copy of the original.
- Flag anything that looks altered, out of sequence, or inconsistent early — better to know about a weak point in your own file than to be surprised by it in cross-examination.

### 4. Common gaps that weaken a case at hearing

- Incidents with no corroborating evidence at all (just a verbal account).
- Undated or unlabeled screenshots — a judge or opposing counsel can't weigh what they can't place in time.
- A pattern that exists in the client's memory but isn't visible in the written record because entries weren't linked to each other.
- Evidence sitting on a device the client no longer has access to (an old phone, a joint account they've been locked out of).

### 5. Pre-hearing checklist

- [ ] Every incident has at least one linked source document or corroborating record
- [ ] Dates are either confirmed or explicitly marked approximate — none are guessed
- [ ] Witnesses are identified with contact information where available
- [ ] Originals (not just screenshots-of-screenshots) are preserved somewhere durable
- [ ] The client has reviewed the chronology for accuracy in their own words

## Kit 2 — Survivor Referral & Consent Readiness Kit (DV organizations / advocates)

**Subtitle:** A short, practical checklist for referring a survivor into a documentation tool — covering consent, safety, and what to say (and not say) at handoff.

### 1. Before you refer — what informed consent should cover

- What the tool is for (organizing her own documentation) and what it is not (it does not replace an attorney, an advocate, or a safety plan).
- That her account and what she writes in it are hers — your organization does not get automatic visibility into her records just because you referred her.
- What she'd be sharing and with whom if she later chooses to share her record with an attorney or an advocate at your org — and that any such sharing is her choice, scoped to what she selects, and revocable by her at any time.
- That documenting is not the same as reporting, and using the tool creates no obligation to file a report, seek a protective order, or take any specific legal step.

### 2. What to tell her about the tool itself, in plain language

- It's a place to record what's happening — a screenshot, a voice note, a few lines about an incident — without needing to organize it herself. It builds a timeline from what she adds.
- It's free for survivors to use.
- She controls what leaves her account. Nothing is shared automatically.
- It is not a crisis line, a safety plan, or a substitute for calling for help in an emergency.

### 3. Handoff checklist — what to say, and what not to promise

- Do explain the "exit safely" / quick-exit option if the tool has one, and how it works on her specific device.
- Do explain that documenting takes time and there's no deadline — she can add one entry today and more later.
- Don't promise a specific legal outcome, that a judge will "believe her" because she documented, or that documentation alone resolves a case.
- Don't ask her to document on a shared or monitored device if avoidable — talk through device safety as part of the referral, not as an afterthought.
- Do let her know she can stop, delete her account, or simply not finish — there's no penalty and no obligation.

### 4. Advocate boundaries worth stating out loud

- You are helping her access a tool, not providing legal advice or making a legal determination about her situation.
- If she asks you whether something "counts" as abuse or would hold up in court, it's appropriate to say that's a question for an attorney, not for you to answer definitively.
- Your role here is logistics and support, not case strategy.

### 5. Pre-referral checklist

- [ ] She understands the tool is hers — not visible to your org unless she shares it
- [ ] She's been told what sharing with an attorney or advocate would actually reveal, in plain terms
- [ ] Device safety has been discussed if she's on a shared or monitored device
- [ ] She knows about any quick-exit/safety feature and how to use it on her device
- [ ] She's been told this is optional and reversible, with no obligation attached
